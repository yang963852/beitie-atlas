# 李斯

## 中文

库里三件秦刻石都归到李斯名下：琅琊、泰山、峄山。三件都因秦始皇二十八年（前 219）东巡而立，秦二世元年（前 209）又在石上加刻。三处馆方著录对他的官称并不一致：中国国家博物馆记随行的是“廷尉”，西安碑林与泰安方面都写作“丞相”。同一年的同一件事，两种写法并存，本页照录，不改写成一种。[中国国家博物馆，琅琊刻石](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml) · [西安碑林博物馆，峄山刻石](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home) · [泰山风景名胜区，岱庙](https://tsgw.taian.gov.cn/art/2021/6/13/art_363479_10320189.html)

读[《琅琊刻石》](../works/langya-keshi.md)可以先从一个“传”字入手。国博的条目说此刻石“书体为秦篆，传为随行廷尉李斯所书”，是传统归名，条目也没有引署名或当时文献作证。现存残石是原石的后半部：高一百三十二点二厘米，宽六十五点八至七十一点三厘米，厚三十六点二厘米，存十三行八十七字。前两行是前 219 年从臣的官职姓名，后十一行是前 209 年二世补刻的诏书。一块石上两代刻辞，字口的漫漶也不同，读的时候要分开记。[中国国家博物馆，琅琊刻石](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml)

[《泰山刻石》](../works/taishan-keshi.md)剩下的更少：原石只存二世诏书的残字十个，在岱庙东御座内，其中“臣去疾臣请矣臣”七字完整，“斯昧死”三字残泐。这十个字在诏书末尾，是从臣奏请的部分；“斯”在这里是李斯的自称，与后世书画的落款不是一回事——石上有名字，不等于自署。始皇的诏文今主要靠拓本流传，石上已经读不到。[泰山风景名胜区，岱庙](https://tsgw.taian.gov.cn/art/2021/6/13/art_363479_10320189.html)

[《峄山刻石》](../works/yishan-inscription.md)在西安碑林，高二百一十八厘米、宽八十四厘米，十五行，满行十五字，两面刻字。它不是秦石：北宋淳化四年（993），郑文宝用徐铉的摹本刻成。碑林的说法是“原为秦丞相李斯所书”——“原为”二字指向那块已经不存的秦石，不是眼前这块宋碑。三条著录放在一起，措辞的松紧与石头的年代恰好相反：原石残块写“传为”，宋人摹刻反而写得更肯定。为什么会这样，条目本身没有解释；但读秦篆时记着这一点，就不容易把重刻的碑当作秦人的手。[西安碑林博物馆，峄山刻石](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

三块石还给同一件事：秦篆的样子。峄山条目说秦篆“用笔圆转，结构匀称，点画粗细均匀”，并引旧评“画如铁石，字若飞动，作楷隶之祖，为不易之法”。这是对书体的描述，不是对李斯其人的判断。拿它当读字的尺子可以，拿它当李斯的个人风格则要留余地——三块石上没有一块能确证是他的手。[西安碑林博物馆，峄山刻石](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

### 生平与身份

生卒、字号与籍贯待考——本库现有来源未记。三条著录记的是刻石与岱庙，不含李斯的小传；“廷尉”与“丞相”两种官称的出入已见上文，两说并存，本页不改写成一种。[中国国家博物馆，琅琊刻石](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml)。据 `dynastySource`，本页把他系于秦。

### 书体与师承

书体与师承待考——本库现有著录未及。峄山条目所记“用笔圆转，结构匀称”是对秦篆这一书体的描述，不是关于他师承的记载。[西安碑林博物馆，峄山刻石](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

### 代表作与本库关联

本库所收秦刻石有[《琅琊刻石》](../works/langya-keshi.md)、[《泰山刻石》](../works/taishan-keshi.md)与[《峄山刻石》](../works/yishan-inscription.md)。三者都以“传”归于李斯，分别存原石残块、二世诏书残字与宋人摹刻，各有其年代与载体，不能合成一件秦人亲笔。

## English

Three Qin inscriptions in this library carry Li Si’s name: Langya, Mount Tai and Mount Yi. All three were set up on the First Emperor’s eastern tour of 219 BCE, and all three were added to in 209 BCE, the first year of the Second Emperor. The museum entries do not agree on his office: the National Museum of China records that the attendant who accompanied the emperor was a commandant of justice, while the Xi’an Beilin Museum and the Mount Tai authority both call him chancellor. Two titles for one event in one year; this page records both and does not reduce them to one. [National Museum of China, Langya inscription](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml) · [Xi’an Beilin Museum, Mount Yi inscription](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home) · [Mount Tai authority, Dai Temple](https://tsgw.taian.gov.cn/art/2021/6/13/art_363479_10320189.html)

The [Langya inscription](../works/langya-keshi.md) is best entered through a single word: “traditionally”. The National Museum’s entry says the script is Qin seal script, “traditionally attributed to the commandant of justice Li Si who accompanied the emperor” — a traditional attribution, and the entry cites neither a signature nor a contemporary document. The surviving block is the rear half of the original: 132.2 cm high, 65.8 to 71.3 cm wide, 36.2 cm thick, thirteen lines and eighty-seven characters. The first two lines give the offices and names of attendants from 219 BCE; the following eleven lines carry the Second Emperor’s edict of 209 BCE. Two reigns on one stone, worn to different degrees — keep the accounts apart as you read. [National Museum of China, Langya inscription](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml)

Less again survives of the [Mount Tai inscription](../works/taishan-keshi.md): ten damaged characters of the Second Emperor’s edict, kept in the eastern throne room of the Dai Temple. Seven of them, “chen quji chen qing yi chen”, are intact; the three reading “Si mei si” are worn. They belong to the close of the edict, where the attending officials submit their request; the “Si” there is Li Si naming himself, which is not the same as a signature on a painting or a scroll. A name on a stone is not an autograph. The First Emperor’s text survives today mainly through rubbings; on the stone it can no longer be read. [Mount Tai authority, Dai Temple](https://tsgw.taian.gov.cn/art/2021/6/13/art_363479_10320189.html)

The [Mount Yi inscription](../works/yishan-inscription.md) is at the Xi’an Beilin Museum: 218 cm high, 84 cm wide, fifteen lines of fifteen characters, carved on both faces. It is not a Qin stone. In 993, the fourth year of Chunhua in the Northern Song, Zheng Wenbao cut it from a tracing of Xu Xuan’s copy. The Beilin entry says it “was originally written by the Qin chancellor Li Si” — “originally” pointing to the lost Qin stone, not to the Song slab in front of you. Put the three entries together and the firmness of the wording runs opposite to the age of the stone: the original fragment says “traditionally”, while the Song recutting speaks more confidently. Why, the entries do not say; but keeping this in mind makes it harder to mistake a recut for a Qin hand. [Xi’an Beilin Museum, Mount Yi inscription](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

All three stones also testify to one thing: what Qin seal script looked like. The Mount Yi entry describes it as “round in the turn of the brush, even in structure, uniform in the thickness of stroke”, and quotes an old judgement: “drawn like iron and stone, the characters seem to fly, the ancestor of regular and clerical script, a law not to be changed.” That is a description of a script, not a judgement of the man. Use it to measure characters; do not use it as Li Si’s personal style — not one of the three stones can be shown to be his hand. [Xi’an Beilin Museum, Mount Yi inscription](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

### Life and identity

Dates, courtesy name and native place remain open: the sources listed for this page do not record them. The three catalogues describe steles and the Dai Temple and carry no biography of Li Si; the two titles, commandant of justice and chancellor, are both kept as above. [National Museum of China, Langya inscription](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202010/t20201028_247940.shtml). By `dynastySource` this page places him in the Qin.

### Scripts and models

Scripts and teacher lineage remain open; the cited catalogues do not address them. The Mount Yi entry's description of Qin seal script — round in the turn of the brush, even in structure — describes a script, not a statement about his teachers. [Xi’an Beilin Museum, Mount Yi inscription](https://www.beilin-museum.com/index.php?a=index&aid=2620&c=View&m=home)

### Works in this library

The library holds the [Langya inscription](../works/langya-keshi.md), [Mount Tai inscription](../works/taishan-keshi.md) and [Mount Yi inscription](../works/yishan-inscription.md). All three are attributed to Li Si by tradition, and they survive respectively as a fragment of the original stone, ten characters of the Second Emperor's edict, and a Song recutting. Each has its own date and carrier; they cannot be merged into one Qin autograph.
