# 礼器碑

## 背景

### 中文

东汉永寿二年（156），碑记鲁相韩敕修孔庙、铸礼器之事。石今藏曲阜汉魏碑刻陈列馆。

### English

An Eastern Han stele of 156 recording the chancellor Han Chi’s repair of the Confucius Temple and the casting of ritual vessels. Held in the Qufu Han–Wei Stele Gallery.

## 导读

### 中文

#### 01 概述

《礼器碑》又称《韩敕碑》《汉鲁相韩敕造孔庙礼器碑》，东汉永寿二年（156）立，鲁相韩敕所立，原在山东曲阜孔庙，今藏曲阜汉魏碑刻陈列馆。[故宫博物院藏明拓本著录](https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/impres/234635.html)（下文简称“故宫著录”）记其“碑高227.2厘米，宽102.4厘米，四面环刻，隶书”；[孔子博物馆的碑刻说明](https://www.confuciusmuseum.cn/news/wb/2023-07-23/2630.html)（下文简称“孔子博物馆说明”）记“碑阳16行，满行36字”。两处对碑阳行款的记载一致。

碑文记韩敕修饰孔庙、造立礼器之事。故宫著录称“碑的内容记录了韩勅修饰孔庙和制作礼器之事宜”；[山东宣传网的“孔庙三碑”专文](https://www.sdxc.gov.cn/whql/whcc/202508/t20250822_16374659.htm)（下文简称“山东宣传网专文”）补出韩敕字叔节、免除孔子舅族颜氏与妻族亓官氏徭役、更作二舆、疏整庙内排水等细节，并引用碑文“修饰宅庙，更作二舆，朝车威熹”数语。

碑阳除序、铭外，还刻韩敕等九人题名；碑阴三列各十七行，左侧三列各四行，右侧四列各四行，均为题名（故宫著录）。这是读此碑时最先要建立的空间感：它不是一块只有正文的碑，而是一组人、一次工程和一份名单刻在同一石上。

所引三处馆藏著录都没有记撰书人姓名。这只是“所核页面未见记载”，不等于历代无说；本文不据此断言此碑从来无书者。

本段待考：

- 碑额：孔子博物馆说明作“有额无字”，故宫著录作“无碑额”。两说并存，本文不取其一。
- 尺寸：仅故宫著录给出，未说明测量时点、是否含座，也未说明是原石现状还是文献所记。
- 韩敕之名，故宫著录作“韩勅”，为一字异写；本文统一作“韩敕”，不改引文。

#### 02 历史与流传

此碑“在唐代不显于世，宋稍显”，至清代被王澍、翁方纲、郭尚先等推为汉碑隶书极则（故宫著录）。原存孔庙同文门，1978 年移入东庑，1998 年移入曲阜汉魏碑刻陈列馆（山东宣传网专文）。

拓本方面，故宫著录的新00044512为明拓本：“黑墨精拓，碑阳、碑阴、碑侧俱全，有张伯英题签，并有‘君言鑑赏’等藏印多方”，并记“绝思”二字未损连。这一句既是版本描述，也是判断拓本先后的字口依据：石面损处尚未连在一起时拓下的字，和后来拓本不是同一种字口。本项目的实物记录见[原石](../catalogue/liqi-bei.md#stele-liqi)与[张伯英题签明拓本](../catalogue/liqi-bei.md#liqi-ming-44512)。

历代著录，故宫列明都穆《金薤琳琅》、清孙承泽《庚子销夏记》、王昶《金石萃编》三种。清人的品评与金石著录在这件碑上几乎同时出现，读流传史时可以把“谁看过石”和“谁拓过纸”分开记。

影响方面，山东宣传网专文记唐隶名家韩择木“早期隶书即取法于《礼器碑》”。这是一条后世的取法记载，不是碑刻当年的信息。

本段待考：

- 宋拓是否存世、明拓各本之间的先后，所核页面没有给出谱系。
- 张伯英题签的年代与题签原文，所核页面只记“有张伯英题签”。
- 1978 年以前的陈列位置变化，所核页面只给“原存同文门”一句。

#### 03 作品赏析

先看可核的形制：四面环刻，碑阳十六行、满行三十六字，碑阴与两侧皆题名（故宫著录、孔子博物馆说明）。杨守敬《平碑记》被故宫著录转述为：此碑“字口完整，碑侧之字锋芒如新”，飘逸多姿、纵横迭宕，为书家所激赏，“攻汉书者，多以《礼器碑》为范本”。

清人的两句评语值得整段抄下。王澍《虚舟题跋》云：“隶法以汉为奇，每碑各出一奇莫有同者，而此碑尤为奇绝，瘦劲如铁，变化若龙，一字一奇，不可端倪。”郭尚先《芳坚馆题跋》云：“汉人书以《韩勅造礼器碑》为第一，超迈雍雅，若卿云在空，万象仰曜，意境当在《史晨》、《乙瑛》、《孔庙》、《曹全》诸石之上，无论他石也。”两段都出自清人，经故宫著录转引，本文未见原书，引用时保留“转引”这一层。

翁方纲有一个影响很大的解释：此碑“奇绝多变”是由于七人书写（故宫著录转述）。这是有出处的意见，不是一份七人名单；本文不据此生成七位书家，也不把“七人”写进作品的 attribution。

孔子博物馆说明另有“被古人推为汉碑‘第一杰作’”一句，未具名。它与王澍、郭尚先的“极则”“第一”属同一类后世品评，姑存其说，不作论证依据。

> 待专业审读：本文只记录清人评语与形制，不对“瘦劲如铁”“变化若龙”作技法拆解，也不判断它与其他汉碑的高下。风格描述与优劣比较须由有书法实践的人审读后补写。

#### 05 临习指南

可引的临习传统有两条：杨守敬称“攻汉书者，多以《礼器碑》为范本”（故宫著录转述）；唐韩择木早期隶书取法此碑（山东宣传网专文）。两条都说明此碑长期被当作学习汉隶的入口，但都没有给出具体课程序列。

选本上，原石在曲阜汉魏碑刻陈列馆，故宫著录的明拓本有碑阳、碑阴、碑侧图版，可用来核对整碑四面与字口。具体影印本、字帖的出版信息，本文未核，不列书名，避免把未见的版本写成推荐。

读帖顺序可以先按石面走：碑阳（序、铭、九人题名）→ 碑阴（三列题名）→ 两侧（题名）。先认行列，再认字；先看整面，再看单字。这个顺序依据的是故宫著录对四面行款的记载，不涉及笔法判断。

### English

#### 01 Overview

The Stele of the Ritual Vessels (Liqi bei), also known as the Han Stele of Chancellor Han Chi's Making of Ritual Vessels for the Confucius Temple, was erected in 156, the second year of Yongshou in the Eastern Han, by Han Chi, chancellor of Lu. It stood originally in the Confucius Temple at Qufu and is now held in the Qufu Han–Wei Stele Gallery. The Palace Museum's entry for its Ming rubbing (hereafter "the Palace Museum entry") records a stone 227.2 cm high and 102.4 cm wide, inscribed on four sides in clerical script; the Confucius Museum's note (hereafter "the Confucius Museum note") gives sixteen columns of thirty-six characters on the front. The two agree on the front's format.

The inscription records Han Chi's repair of the Confucius Temple and his casting of ritual vessels. The Palace Museum entry says the text "records Han Chi's refurbishment of the Confucius Temple and the making of ritual vessels". The Shandong Propaganda Network article on the "three steles of the Confucius Temple" (hereafter "the Shandong article") adds that Han Chi, styled Shujie, exempted the Yan clan, relatives of Confucius's mother, and the Qiguan clan, relatives of his wife, from corvée; had two carriages made; and cleared the temple's drainage. It quotes the inscription's phrases "修饰宅庙，更作二舆，朝车威熹" on the repair of the residence and temple and the making of two carriages.

Beyond the preface and the hymn, the front also carries the names of nine men including Han Chi; the back is in three columns of seventeen lines, the left side in three columns of four lines and the right in four columns of four, all of them names (Palace Museum entry). This is the first spatial fact to establish: the stele is not a text alone, but a group of people, a project and a roster cut into one stone.

None of the three cited museum entries records a writer or calligrapher. That means only that the consulted pages do not name one; it does not establish that no attribution ever existed. This article does not conclude that the stele never had a scribe.

Open questions:

- The head: the Confucius Museum note says "有额无字" (a head with no characters); the Palace Museum entry says "无碑额" (no head). Both readings are kept; this article chooses neither.
- Dimensions: only the Palace Museum entry gives them. It does not state when they were taken, whether the base is included, or whether they describe the stone today or a documentary figure.
- The name: the Palace Museum writes 韩勅 for 韩敕, a variant character. This article uses 韩敕 and leaves quotations unchanged.

#### 02 History and transmission

The stele "was not prominent in the Tang and became slightly known in the Song"; in the Qing it was promoted as the supreme norm of Han clerical script by Wang Shu, Weng Fanggang and Guo Shangxian (Palace Museum entry). It stood originally at the Tongwen Gate of the Confucius Temple, was moved into the east wing in 1978, and into the Qufu Han–Wei Stele Gallery in 1998 (Shandong article).

For rubbings, the Palace Museum's 新00044512 is a Ming impression: "a fine black-ink rubbing, complete in front, back and both sides, with a title slip by Zhang Boying and several collectors' seals including 'Junyan jianshang'". The entry also notes that the two characters 绝思 are "not yet damaged into a joined stroke". That sentence is both a description of the edition and a criterion for dating impressions: characters taken before the damaged areas joined are not the same as those in later rubbings. This project's object records are the [original stone](../catalogue/liqi-bei.md#stele-liqi) and the [Ming rubbing with Zhang Boying's title slip](../catalogue/liqi-bei.md#liqi-ming-44512).

For the bibliographic record, the Palace Museum lists Du Mu's Jinshi linlang (Ming), Sun Chengze's Gengzi xiaoxia ji (Qing) and Wang Chang's Jinshi cuibian (Qing). In the Qing, connoisseurship and epigraphy converged on this stele; when reading its transmission, keep "who saw the stone" and "who took a rubbing" as separate records.

On influence, the Shandong article records that the Tang clerical master Han Zemu "took the Liqi bei as his early model". This is a later record of reception, not information from the year of carving.

Open questions:

- Whether a Song rubbing survives, and the order of Ming impressions: the consulted pages give no stemma.
- The date and wording of Zhang Boying's title slip: the entry says only that one exists.
- Changes of display before 1978: the consulted page says only "originally at the Tongwen Gate".

#### 03 Reading the work

Begin with verifiable format: four inscribed sides, sixteen columns of thirty-six characters on the front, names on the back and both sides (Palace Museum entry; Confucius Museum note). The Palace Museum paraphrases Yang Shoujing's Pingbei ji: the cuts are intact, those on the sides still sharp; the writing is "graceful and varied, rising and falling", admired by calligraphers, and "those who study Han clerical script mostly take the Liqi bei as their model".

Two Qing judgements deserve full quotation. Wang Shu, in Xuzhou tiba: "In clerical method Han is the wonder; every stele brings out a wonder of its own and none repeats another, yet this one is the most extraordinary: lean and strong as iron, changing like a dragon, every single character a wonder whose limits cannot be fathomed." Guo Shangxian, in Fangjian guan tiba: "Among Han writers, the Han Chi Stele of the Ritual Vessels stands first: transcendent and serene, as if auspicious clouds filled the sky and all phenomena looked up to their radiance; its realm must lie above the Shi Chen, Yi Ying, Kongmiao and Cao Quan steles, to say nothing of the rest." Both are Qing texts quoted through the Palace Museum entry; this article has not seen the original books, and keeps the layer of quotation explicit.

Weng Fanggang offered an influential explanation: the stele's extraordinary variety comes from seven men writing it (paraphrased in the Palace Museum entry). This is a sourced opinion, not a roster of seven men; this article neither turns it into seven calligraphers nor writes "seven" into the work's attribution.

The Confucius Museum note adds that the stele was "pushed by the ancients to be the 'first masterpiece' among Han steles", without naming them. It belongs with Wang Shu's and Guo Shangxian's "supreme norm" and "first" as later evaluation; it is recorded here but not used as proof.

> Pending specialist review: this article records Qing judgements and format only. It does not dissect "lean as iron" or "changing like a dragon" into technique, and does not rank the stele against other Han steles. Stylistic description and comparison must be written by someone with calligraphic practice after review.

#### 05 Copying guide

Two documented traditions can be cited: Yang Shoujing's statement that "those who study Han clerical script mostly take the Liqi bei as their model" (paraphrased in the Palace Museum entry), and Han Zemu's early modelling on the stele (Shandong article). Both show the stele's long use as an entry to Han clerical script, but neither gives a course sequence.

For editions, the stone is in the Qufu Han–Wei Stele Gallery, and the Palace Museum's Ming rubbing has plates of the front, back and both sides for checking the four faces and the cuts. This article has not verified the publication data of modern facsimiles or copybooks, so it names none, rather than presenting an unseen edition as a recommendation.

A reading order can follow the stone: front (preface, hymn, nine names), then back (three columns of names), then both sides (names). Establish columns first, then characters; the whole face first, then single characters. This order rests on the Palace Museum's account of the four faces, not on any judgement of brushwork.

#### 06 Further study

- **The three steles of the Confucius Temple**: the [Yi Ying Stele](../works/yiying-bei.md) was erected in 153 and the [Shi Chen Stele](../works/shichen-bei.md) in 169; both stand in the same temple, thirteen years apart from this one (Shandong article). Compare format, donor lists and reasons for carving; stylistic differences follow the review rule in 03 and are not concluded here.
- **Other Han clerical types**: the [Cao Quan Stele](../works/cao-quan-bei.md), [Zhang Qian Stele](../works/zhangqian-bei.md), [Shimen Song](../works/shimen-song.md) and [Xixia Song](../works/xixia-song.md) are all recorded and can serve as comparisons. Record each stone's condition and format before discussing handling.
- **The formation of clerical script**: the [Shuihudi Qin slips](../works/shuihudi-qinjian.md) and the [Wufeng inscription](../works/wufeng-inscription.md) predate this stele and can serve as references for the formative period; the precise development needs a history-of-calligraphy source and is not argued here.
- **Later clerical script**: the [Xiping stone classics](../works/xiping-stone-classics.md) (eight-tenths clerical), the [Shitai Xiaojing](../works/shitai-xiaojing.md) (Tang eight-tenths clerical) and the [five-character clerical scroll](../works/yi-wuzi.md) (Qing) show the script in different periods, useful for observing "one script, different periods".
- **Museum exhibition**: the Confucius Museum's 2023 article mentions an exhibition of fifty rubbings, "Root and Soul". Its current status has not been checked; consult the museum before travelling.
